PRIMER: reviewing a recommender's ranking feed by feed

THE TASK
Avito is a classifieds marketplace. A "feed" is one request to its main-page recommendation feed: one user, one
moment, 5 to 300 candidate listings. The data never shows titles, prices or images; a listing is a category plus
numbers. We must order the candidates so the ones the user engaged with come first. "Engaged" (the label, "positive")
means: after this feed was shown, the user started an engagement with that listing from this feed, in 83-87% of cases
by opening its item card. 74% of feeds have exactly one positive; the mean is 1.53 positives per feed.

The metric is NDCG over the full list, averaged over feeds. A positive at position p earns 1/log2(p+1):
p=1: 1.000, p=2: 0.631, p=3: 0.500, p=5: 0.387, p=10: 0.289, p=20: 0.228, p=50: 0.176, p=100: 0.150.
Divided by the ideal (all positives on top). Example: a single positive at rank 20 scores 0.228; moving it to rank 1
gives 1.000 (+0.77). But moving a NEGATIVE to rank 1 pushes every item above its old place down by one, and if the
true positive was at rank 1 the feed falls from 1.000 to 0.631 (-0.37).

These validation and test feeds were shown to users in RANDOM order, so position on screen did not influence clicks.

THE MODEL WHOSE RANKING YOU SEE ("FD16P20")
An ensemble of 20 neural rankers trained on 3.1 million earlier feeds (246 million candidates), then fine-tuned on
the validation days. Each ranker reads every column on the card and much more: the full 128-dimensional vectors of
the user, of every candidate and of all up to 256 history events, attention from each candidate to every history
event, the 16 most similar history items, popularity counts, and a self-supervised model of history sequences.
It scores 0.4027 NDCG on validation (random order: 0.289; best single feature: about 0.35).
So the model already "knows" every simple relation between the columns you see. A change helps only when you
can reason about something the model plausibly gets wrong.

HOW GOOD THE MODEL'S ORDER IS (measured on 13,701 labelled validation feeds)
Positive rate of the item at a given model rank:
  rank 1: 13.1% | rank 2: 9.2% | rank 3: 7.7% | ranks 4-5: 6.2% | 6-10: 4.4% | 11-20: 2.9% | 21-50: 1.6% |
  51-100: 0.85% | 101-300: 0.35%      (average candidate: 1.6%)
Where the feed's best-ranked positive sits: rank 1 in 13% of feeds, ranks 2-3 in 15%, 4-10 in 26%, 11-30 in 25%,
31 or lower in 20%.
Consequence: moving an item to rank 1 pays off on average only if you believe it is more than about 13% likely to
be positive, i.e. 8 times the rate of a typical item at ranks 21-50. Moving one item from rank 40 to rank 5 pays
only if it beats about 6%. Most apparent "mistakes" are not mistakes: clicks are noisy and often exploratory.

THE CARD
Header: number of candidates, history length (max 256), logged-in or guest (user 0), whether the user vector is
missing, weekday and Moscow hour.
HISTORY newest first: the user's last 40 events as Category/event-type@source, consecutive repeats as "x n".
  Event types: view = item card viewed; contact1 = first contact with the seller about the item; contact =
  repeatable contact (phone reveal, call, message); fav = add/remove favourite; order / orderstep = delivery or
  purchase flow (goods categories only); lead = lead-type action; done = completion of a lead or contact;
  guestview = view marker of a guest. Sources (only for view, fav, lead): feed = this main-page feed,
  search = search or catalogue, similar = "similar listings" block, favlist = favourites list, goods, rec2 = a
  second recommendation surface, realty = real-estate surface, ? = unknown.
  The history has no timestamps, only order. Its old end is thinned (views are dropped first), so long histories
  over-represent contacts. Items the user already viewed are REMOVED from the candidates, so a candidate is
  never an item from the history; a near-duplicate (cosine 0.99+) is usually a similar listing or a re-post.
HISTORY by category: per category, views (v) and other actions (a) over the whole history, and @k = index of the
  newest event in that category (0 = the newest event of the history).
CANDIDATES, one line each, in the model's order (best first):
  rank | z = model score (within-feed z-score; gaps between neighbours show how sure the model is)
  | category
  | f3 = affinity of the user to the candidate's leaf category (0..1; one value per category within a feed)
  | f1 = finer affinity inside the category (0..1; at its floor .02 when there is no affinity)
  | f2 = affinity of the user to the listing's region (0 = no activity there; "H" marks the feed's most common
  value, i.e. the user's home region)
  | f4 = age of the listing (0..1, HIGH = OLD). On this random-order traffic the freshest tenth (lowest f4) is
  clicked 0.46 times the average; the rest are within 0.9-1.13 times.
  | f5 = engagement rate of the listing, unsmoothed (noisy when the listing has few impressions; 0 = no data)
  | ucos = cosine between the user vector and the item vector (Avito's own two-tower embeddings; "-" = missing)
  | hmax = largest cosine between this candidate and any history item, "@k:type" = index (0 = newest) and event
  type of that history event. A candidate that nearly duplicates a RECENT history item (0.99+) is positive about
  2.8 times the base rate; the model knows this.
  | h10 = largest cosine to the newest 10 history events
  | cathist = the user's history in this candidate's category: views v, other actions a, @k newest index; "-" = none
  | pop = how often the listing appears in other users' histories (popularity, leave-user-out)
  | exp = how often it was shown as a candidate in the training period (0 = new or cold listing)

WHAT WE KNOW FROM ERROR ANALYSIS
- Recency of interest matters most: similarity to the newest few events predicts clicks better than similarity
  to the whole history.
- About 40% of positives ranked in the lower half are in a category absent from the user's recent 32 events
  (exploration). The model ranks these low, and on average that is correct: such categories are clicked half
  as often. Nobody has found a way to tell which feeds are exploratory (best detector AUC 0.71-0.75 did not help).
- Inside the top category, the clicked item often loses to other items on every column (cosine, f1, f3,
  popularity, age). Among near-duplicate candidates, f1, f4 and f5 are at chance for telling the clicked one.
- Train click-through rates are worthless here; popularity in histories (pop) carries some signal.
- Guests (user 0) click at about the same rate as logged-in users (1.47% vs 1.59% per candidate).
- Large feeds (100+ candidates) carry two thirds of the lost NDCG.
